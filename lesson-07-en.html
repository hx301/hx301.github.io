Waves, Tides, and the Coast

Session 7 · 2026-10-30 (online module 2) · Textbook Ch.8 §8.2–8.8

Driving question: Why do waves, tides, and estuarine circulation need different explanations?

Class notes

A wave propagates a disturbance and energy; water-particle motion is not the same as the speed of a crest. Surface gravity waves use gravity as the restoring force; deep- or shallow-water behavior depends on the ratio of depth to wavelength.

For linear, non-rotating shallow-water long waves, \(c\approx \sqrt{gH}\). Here “shallow” is relative to wavelength, not simply a small depth on a chart.

Stable stratification supports internal waves; tides are forced periodically by the Moon and Sun, but local range and currents also depend on basin shape, depth, rotation, friction, and resonance. Estuaries often export fresher water above a compensating inflow of saltier water, but tidal mixing, runoff, and geometry change the stratification.

  • Distinguish phase speed, group velocity, and water-particle motion.
  • Spring tides relate to aligned lunar and solar forcing, not to the season.
  • A current at one instant does not represent the long-term net transport.

In my own words

I used to think a rapidly moving crest meant the same parcel of water rushed all the way to shore. Later I saw that in small-amplitude waves the particles mostly orbit near a mean position while the crest advances as a phase; breaking and net drift near the shore are a different problem.

What I did not understand + How I worked it out

What I did not understand at first: why is the phase-speed ratio 2 for the same long wave in 100 m and 25 m of water?
What I later worked out: for shallow-water long waves \(c\approx \sqrt{gH}\), so the ratio is \(\sqrt{100/25}=2\); “shallow” is relative to wavelength, not a small depth on a chart.
