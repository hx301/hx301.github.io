Geostrophy, Ekman, and Potential Vorticity

Chapter 7 · Textbook Ch.7 §7.2–7.8 · Talley et al. (2011), 6th ed.

\[f=2\Omega\sin\varphi\]
\[u_g=-\frac{g}{f}\eta_y,\qquad v_g=\frac{g}{f}\eta_x\]
\[V_{\mathrm{Ek}}=\frac{\tau}{\rho_0f}\]
\[q=\frac{\zeta+f}{H}\]

Textbook Ch.7 §7.2–7.8; S7 supplement

My explanation

Geostrophy is the leading balance between the pressure gradient and Coriolis force; density gradients give thermal-wind shear; Ekman transport is the surface response to wind stress; potential vorticity combines layer thickness, relative vorticity, and planetary vorticity in one conservation relation.

In depth: limits of geostrophy and potential vorticity

A sea-surface slope implies a horizontal pressure gradient. In the Northern Hemisphere \(f > 0\), an eastward rise gives northward \(v_g\); the pressure-gradient force points west and the Coriolis acceleration east. Velocity and acceleration have different units; do not add a velocity arrow as a force.

In \(q=(\zeta +f)/H\), relative vorticity, planetary vorticity, and layer thickness jointly set the layer-model PV. With no friction or external PV sources and under the layer assumptions, stretching requires absolute vorticity to increase to preserve the ratio; PV conservation does not mean local \(\zeta \) is fixed.

Gulf Stream section (S7 Fig. S7.20, PDF p.37; Table S7.3, p.38): station spacing 78.0 km, latitude 38.65°N, surface eastward velocity \(94.83 \mathrm{cm}/s\); the calculation assumes no motion at 3,000 m, so the curve is not a direct current-meter measurement.
