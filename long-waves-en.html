One sentence

“Long wave” in the ocean is not one wave but a family: gravity waves, Rossby waves, Kelvin waves, equatorial waves… The first question is: what force restores the water?

Treating “long wave” as one thing mixes tides, tsunamis, Rossby waves and ENSO. Start with the restoring force, then add rotation, stratification and boundaries.

Two meanings of “long wave”

(1) Shallow-water long wave: wavelength ≫ depth, c=√(gH), non-dispersive; tides, tsunami, seiche.

(2) Long wave in physical oceanography: wavelengths of hundreds to thousands of km and periods of days to years; Rossby, Kelvin, equatorial waves.

“Long” depends on what you compare it with: depth, stratification, or Earth's rotation.

Classification tree: restoring force first, boundary second

Ocean long waves

Gravity waves

Restoring force: gravity / buoyancy
  • Surface long waves: tides, tsunami, seiche
  • Internal gravity waves: internal tides, SCS waves
Fast: minutes–days; c=√(gH) or √(g'H)

Rotation waves

Restoring force: β / potential vorticity
  • Planetary Rossby wave
  • Topographic Rossby / shelf wave
Slow: weeks–years; Rossby phase speed is westward

Boundary-trapped waves

Rotation + boundary (coast/equator)
  • Coastal Kelvin wave
  • Equatorial Kelvin wave
  • Equatorial Rossby wave
  • Mixed Rossby–gravity wave
Tied to a coast or the equator; propagate along the boundary

Barotropic vs baroclinic is another axis: barotropic is in phase through the column with a strong surface signal; baroclinic involves density-surface displacement, weak at the surface but strong in the interior.

Three main lines for classification

Gravity vs rotation

Gravity waves are fast (minutes–days); rotation waves are slow (weeks–years). Gravity waves can be non-dispersive; Rossby waves are dispersive.

Barotropic vs baroclinic

Barotropic = whole column in phase, strong surface signal; baroclinic = density-surface displacement, weak surface signal but strong interior.

Free vs trapped

Free waves travel far; trapped waves are tied to a coast or the equator and propagate along the boundary.

A few formulas that are enough

Shallow-water long wave

c = √(gH)

Non-dispersive; tides, tsunami, seiche. H is depth.

Internal wave

c = √(g'H), g' = g Δρ/ρ

Baroclinic; small surface signal, large density-surface displacement.

Planetary Rossby wave

ω = −βk/(k²+l²), c_x = ω/k < 0

Phase speed westward; long waves faster, short waves slower.

Kelvin wave

c = √(gH)

Geostrophic balance normal to the boundary; trapped within a deformation radius.

Equatorial waves

c = √(gH), with the equatorial deformation radius

Kelvin waves eastward, Rossby waves westward.

Five-step decision flow

Given a “long-wave” phenomenon, ask five questions in order:

  1. Wavelength ≫ depth? → shallow-water gravity wave (tide / tsunami / seiche).
  2. Stratification important? → internal wave / baroclinic (internal tides, SCS waves).
  3. Earth's rotation important? → rotation wave (Rossby / Kelvin).
  4. A coast or the equator? → trapped wave (coastal/equatorial Kelvin, equatorial Rossby).
  5. Topography important? → topographic Rossby / shelf wave.

Common pitfalls

  • “Long wave” is not one wave. First ask the restoring force: gravity, buoyancy, β, or a boundary?
  • A westward Rossby phase speed does not mean energy only goes west. The group velocity of short waves can be eastward.
  • A Kelvin wave is not an ordinary gravity wave. It is a geostrophic-balanced wave shaped by rotation and a boundary.
  • Tides are long waves, but their period is set by astronomical forcing. They are forced, not free.
  • A tsunami is a shallow-water long wave in the deep ocean: fast and low-amplitude; it amplifies only as it shoals.

30-second retell

  1. What is the first question in classifying a long wave?
  2. Why do Rossby waves propagate westward?
  3. Why is a Kelvin wave tied to a boundary?

If you can answer these three in your own words, the classification is no longer a list of names.
