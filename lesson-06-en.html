Dynamics: Geostrophy, Ekman, and Potential Vorticity

Session 6 · 2026-10-29 · Textbook Ch.7 §7.2–7.8

Driving question: Why do ocean currents often follow sea-level contours instead of flowing directly downhill?

Class notes

A horizontal pressure gradient first accelerates water, and the Coriolis force grows with speed and turns the motion. At large scales, weak friction, and slow evolution, the two approximately balance and produce geostrophic flow. With x east and y north: \(u=-(g/f)\eta _y\), \(v=(g/f)\eta _x\).

Horizontal density differences change the pressure gradient with depth through hydrostatic balance, producing thermal-wind shear; density gives relative velocity, while absolute velocity needs a reference level or observations.

Wind stress drives Ekman transport in the surface layer; wind-stress curl produces vertical motion at the base of the Ekman layer, stretching or compressing interior columns and linking to Sverdrup meridional transport and western boundary currents. Potential vorticity \(q=(\zeta +f)/h\) is approximately conserved in an ideal, single-layer, frictionless, adiabatic setting.

  • The Coriolis force is always perpendicular to motion and does no work.
  • Surface Ekman flow is deflected 45°; the vertically integrated transport is deflected 90°.
  • Western boundary currents (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio) help close the basin-scale circulation.

In my own words

I used to think the Coriolis force was a real force. Later I saw that it appears only in a rotating frame, is always perpendicular to motion, and does no work; it changes direction, not speed.

What I did not understand + How I worked it out

What I did not understand at first: why is Ekman transport 90° to the wind while the surface flow is only 45°?
What I later worked out: 45° is the surface velocity; integrating the whole Ekman layer cancels much of the deflection at different depths, leaving a net transport at 90°.
