Ocean Waves: Seven Thinking Questions

From “when can Coriolis be ignored” to “why a tsunami crosses an ocean”—seven questions, one chain of wave physics.

Framework: four criteria

All seven questions ask the same thing: at what scale do gravity, rotation, stratification and boundaries take over?

Break it into four criteria; each question below is an application of one of them.

\(\sigma / f\)Is rotation important? ≫ 1 no, ~1 yes
\(kR_{d}\)Wavelength vs deformation radius: ≫ 1 short, ≪ 1 long
kHShallow-water criterion: ≪ 1 gives \(c = \sqrt{gH}\)
\(f < \sigma < N\)Frequency window for free internal waves

The chain: Q1 asks whether f can be dropped; Q2 asks what sets dispersion; Q3–Q4 ask how boundaries trap waves; Q5 asks how a boundary turns a traveling wave into a standing one; Q6 adds stratification plus a disturbance; Q7 is the “long but high-frequency” extreme.

01 · Coriolis: when can it be dropped

01

Under what conditions can Coriolis be neglected for gravity waves?

When \(\sigma \gg f\)—period much shorter than the inertial period, wavelength much smaller than the deformation radius.

The rotating shallow-water dispersion relation is

\[\sigma^2=f^2+gHk^2\quad\text{(long waves)}\]
\[\text{General depth: }\sigma^2=f^2+gk\tanh(kH)\]
\[\text{Without rotation: }\sigma^2=gHk^2\]

The relative importance of Coriolis is about \((f/\sigma )^{2}\). “Ignoring Coriolis” therefore gives two equivalent criteria:

Time criterion

\(T \ll 2\pi /f = 12 h / \sin \varphi \). At mid-latitudes the inertial period is about 14–24 h; wind sea, swell and tsunami with periods of minutes to a few hours all satisfy this.

Space criterion

\(\lambda \ll 2\pi R_{d}\), \(R_{d} = \sqrt{gH}/f\). Barotropic \(R_{d} \approx 2000 \mathrm{km}\); the first baroclinic mode is 25–50 km; that is, \(kR_{d} \gg 1\).

  • Low latitudes: \(f \to 0\) at the equator, so Coriolis itself vanishes; but \(\beta \) takes over and long waves become equatorial or Rossby waves.
  • Small basins: if the basin size \(L \ll R_{d}\)(crossing time much shorter than the inertial period), rotation can also be dropped—e.g., a seiche in a small lake.

Physical picture: Coriolis deflects water parcels and turns a back-and-forth oscillation into an ellipse or rotation. When the oscillation is fast, a parcel has no time to be deflected before the wave has passed—Coriolis is not “small”, it is “too slow”.

Example: wind waves with \(T \approx 3 s\) have \(\sigma /f \approx 10^{4}\) and are non-rotating; the M2 tide at 45°N has \(T = 12.4 h\) and \(\sigma /f \approx 1.4\), so rotation is essential (Kelvin waves, amphidromic points).

02 · Three waves: who is dispersive

02

Are gravity, inertia-gravity and Rossby waves dispersive? Why?

In general all three are dispersive, but each has a non-dispersive limit: shallow-water gravity, short inertia-gravity, long Rossby.

First agree on terms: dispersion means the phase speed \(c = \sigma /k\) depends on wavelength, so a wave packet spreads; equivalently \(c \ne c_{g}\) and \(c_{g}\) depends on k.

Surface gravity waves

Deep water

\[\sigma^2=gk\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c=\sqrt{\frac gk},\quad c_g=\frac c2\]

Strongly dispersive. Long swell arrives first and short waves later—the classic dispersion of swell.

Shallow water \((\lambda \gg H)\)

\[\sigma=k\sqrt{gH}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c=c_g=\sqrt{gH}\]

Non-dispersive. This is why tides and tsunamis keep their shape in shallow water.

So whether a gravity wave is dispersive depends on depth versus wavelength: deep water is dispersive, shallow-water long waves are not. With surface tension, \(\sigma ^{2} = gk + \gamma k^{3}/\rho \): still dispersive, with a minimum phase speed.

Inertia-gravity waves

\[\sigma^2=f^2+gHk^2\]
\[c=\sqrt{gH+\frac{f^2}{k^2}},\quad c_g=\frac{gHk}{\sigma},\quad cc_g=gH\]

Generally dispersive. Two limits: for \(kR \gg 1\), \(\sigma \approx k\sqrt{gH}\) and the wave approaches the non-dispersive limit; for \(kR \ll 1\), \(\sigma \to f\), the phase speed formally diverges, the group velocity goes to zero and energy barely propagates—extremely dispersive.

It also has a lower frequency bound f: for \(\sigma < f\) it cannot propagate freely and the motion becomes geostrophic or Rossby-like. Inertia-gravity waves thus act as a high-pass filter.

Rossby waves

\[\sigma=-\frac{\beta k}{k^2+l^2+R^{-2}},\quad c_x=-\frac{\beta}{k^2+l^2+R^{-2}}\]
\[R=\frac{\sqrt{gH}}f\quad\text{(barotropic)}\]
\[R_n=\frac{c_n}f\quad\text{(baroclinic modes)}\]

Dispersive, and anomalously so: the longer the wave, the faster it travels—long waves go west quickly, short waves slowly. The group velocity is

\[c_{gx}=\frac{\beta(k^2-l^2-R^{-2})}{(k^2+l^2+R^{-2})^2}\]

For long waves \((kR \ll 1)\) the group velocity is also westward; for short waves (large k) it can be eastward. Key limit: as \(kR \ll 1\), \(\sigma \approx -\beta R^{2}k\) and \(c = -\beta R^{2}\) independent of k, so the wave becomes non-dispersive—this is why “long Rossby waves” in ENSO theory can carry a signal westward without much distortion.

Summary: all three are dispersive in general, but each has a non-dispersive limit: shallow-water gravity, short inertia-gravity, long Rossby.

03 · The equator: a waveguide for waves

03

Why does the equatorial wave system form a waveguide? Which waves can it support?

Because \(f = 0\) and changes sign at the equator while \(\beta \) provides the restoring force; waves are trapped within about \(\sqrt{c/\beta }\) of the equator.

Why the equator is a waveguide

  1. \(f = 0\) and changes sign. At the equator the Coriolis parameter vanishes; toward the poles \(f = \beta y\) grows and reverses. A wave of frequency \(\sigma \) can only propagate where \(|f(y)| < \sigma \), i.e. \(|y| < \sigma /\beta \); farther poleward it becomes evanescent and is totally internally reflected.
  2. \(\beta \) provides the restoring force. A parcel displaced meridionally feels the \(\beta y\) term, which pulls it back and sets up a meridional oscillation.
  3. Mathematically, a trapped harmonic oscillator. Separating variables on the equatorial \(\beta \)-plane leaves a harmonic-oscillator equation in y; the solutions are Hermite functions times a Gaussian envelope whose width is the equatorial Rossby deformation radius \(L_{eq} = \sqrt{c/\beta }\).
  4. No steady geostrophic balance. Because f changes sign, large-scale disturbances cannot settle into geostrophy; they must propagate as waves, and eastern/western boundaries reflect them, completing the wave system.
\[V_n\propto H_n\!\left(\frac y{L_{\mathrm{eq}}}\right)e^{-y^2/(2L_{\mathrm{eq}}^2)},\quad L_{\mathrm{eq}}=\sqrt{\frac c\beta}\]

Scales: barotropic \(c \approx 200 m/s \to L_{eq} \approx 3000 \mathrm{km}\); first baroclinic mode \(c \approx 2.5 m/s \to L_{eq} \approx 300 \mathrm{km}\). Waves are confined to a few \(L_{eq}\).

One sentence: the equator is where rotation is weakest and \(\beta \) is cleanest; waves cannot escape meridionally and cannot settle into geostrophy, so they travel along the equator.

04 · Shelf waves: tied to the coast

04

What kinds of shelf long waves are there, and how do they propagate?

Mainly Kelvin, shelf and edge waves, plus the double Kelvin wave; all are trapped by the coast and topography, with the coast (and shallow water) on the right in the NH.

KKelvin wave

Restoring force: gravity + Coriolis, with geostrophic balance normal to the coast. \(\sigma = kc\), \(c = \sqrt{gH}\), non-dispersive; cross-shore velocity \(v = 0\), amplitude decays offshore as \(e^{-x/R}\) with \(R = c/f\).

SShelf wave

A topographic Rossby wave. Restoring force: potential-vorticity conservation over a sloping bottom, \(\sigma < f\)(sub-inertial), generally dispersive; amplitude concentrated over the shelf/slope, decaying offshore, with multiple modes.

EEdge wave

Trapped by the beach slope and able to travel near the shoreline; Stokes edge waves (super-inertial) and sub-inertial edge waves matter for run-up and beach morphology.

DDouble Kelvin wave

Propagates along a depth step (shelf break), decaying exponentially on both sides; unidirectional, with a speed between \(\sqrt{gH_{1}}\) and \(\sqrt{gH_{2}}\).

Common propagation features

  • Energy travels along isobaths or the coastline, trapped by topography and the coast, decaying offshore.
  • In the NH the coast is on the right, and so is shallow water: poleward along eastern boundaries (west coasts of continents), equatorward along western boundaries (east coasts); reversed in the SH.
  • Dispersion differs: Kelvin waves are non-dispersive, shelf waves generally dispersive, yet both travel in the same alongshore direction.
  • They are the coastal signal highway: storms, tides and ENSO signals propagate along the coast this way; friction, scattering and changing bathymetry damp and deform them.
One sentence: Kelvin waves are trapped by “coast + geostrophy”, shelf waves by “slope + potential vorticity”; the first keeps its shape, the second disperses, but both are tied to the same coastline.

05 · Bay seiche: a gravity wave put in a box

05

How is a bay seiche similar to and different from a gravity wave?

A seiche is a standing gravity wave enclosed by boundaries; a gravity wave is a traveling wave in open water. Same restoring force, different waveform, energy flux and period structure.

A seiche is a standing free oscillation of an enclosed or semi-enclosed water body (lake, bay, harbour), excited by wind, air-pressure jumps, earthquakes or tides—a resonant response to forcing.

Closed basin

\[T_n=\frac{2L}{n\sqrt{gH}}\]

Antinodes at both ends with nodes in between; the fundamental is \(n = 1\).

Bay open at one end

\[T_n=\frac{4L}{(2n-1)\sqrt{gH}}\]

The open end is approximately a node and the closed end an antinode; this is Merian’s formula.

Similarities

  • Both are surface gravity waves with gravity as the restoring force; for \(\lambda \gg H\) both obey the shallow-water equations with \(c = \sqrt{gH}\).
  • A seiche is the superposition of two oppositely traveling gravity waves, so it is a special form of gravity wave.
  • Both have nodes and antinodes, can resonate, and are damped by friction; at large scales both feel rotation (Kelvin-wave seiches, amphidromic points).

Differences

Bay seicheOpen-ocean gravity wave
WaveformStanding wave, fixed nodes/antinodesTraveling wave, pattern propagates
EnergyZero net energy flux (except forcing/damping)Non-zero net flux, directed transport
PeriodDiscrete eigenperiods set by basin geometryContinuous spectrum set by the source

Formula\(T = 2L/\sqrt{gH}\) or \(4L/\sqrt{gH}\)\(T = \lambda /\sqrt{gH}\), with \(\lambda \) set by the source

Why the phase differs: a standing wave can be written \(\eta = A \cos (kx) \cos (\sigma t)\); the momentum equation then gives \(u \propto \sin (kx) \sin (\sigma t)\), exactly 90° out of phase in time. For a traveling wave \(\eta \) and u are in phase, so the velocity is largest at the crest.

One sentence: a seiche is a gravity wave trapped by boundaries and turned into a standing wave; a gravity wave is a traveling wave in open water.

06 · Internal waves: stratification + disturbance

06

What are the basic conditions for internal waves to occur?

Three conditions: stable stratification, a disturbance that displaces isopycnals, and a frequency inside \(f < \sigma < N\).

① Stable density stratification

Density increases with depth (light over heavy), i.e.

\[N^2=-\frac g{\rho_0}\frac{\mathrm d\rho}{\mathrm dz}>0\]

This is the source of the buoyancy restoring force. Without stratification there are no internal waves; in the real ocean the thermocline or halocline provides strong stratification.

② A disturbance that displaces isopycnals

  • Barotropic tides over rough topography → internal tides;
  • Wind stress → near-inertial waves (especially in the mixed layer);
  • Geostrophic adjustment → fronts, eddies, convection;
  • Shear instability, baroclinic instability;
  • Nonlinear interactions of surface waves;
  • River plumes, submarine landslides, earthquakes.

③ Frequency inside the window

\[f<\sigma<N\]
\[\text{Continuously stratified internal waves:}\]
\[\sigma^2=\frac{N^2k_h^2+f^2m^2}{k_h^2+m^2}\]

For \(\sigma > N\) or \(\sigma < f\) the wave becomes evanescent and cannot propagate freely (for \(\sigma < f\) the motion becomes geostrophic or Rossby-like).

Additional conditions

  • Interfacial waves (two-layer fluid) need a pycnocline and an interfacial disturbance; internal solitary waves also need a balance of nonlinearity and dispersion (KdV) and a finite amplitude.
  • Background flow Doppler-shifts the frequency and creates critical layers; strong shear can make internal waves unstable \((\mathrm{Ri} < 1/4)\) and break them.
  • Horizontal and vertical scales are constrained by stratification, depth and the Rossby radius; the first baroclinic mode is the most common.
One sentence: stratification is the “medium” condition, a disturbance is the “excitation” condition, and \(f < \sigma < N\) is the “propagation” condition.

07 · Tsunami: a long wave, not a low frequency

07

How does a tsunami form? Is its long-distance propagation affected by Coriolis? Why?

Seafloor displacement radiates a shallow-water long wave; over long distances Coriolis is negligible because \(\sigma \gg f\) and \(kR_{d} \gg 1\).

Generation

  1. Source: a shallow submarine earthquake on a thrust fault displaces the seafloor vertically over a large area; landslides, volcanic flank collapse, eruptions and impacts also work.
  2. Long-wave radiation: the displaced water column restores under gravity and radiates long gravity waves. The source is tens to hundreds of km across—much larger than the depth (~4 km)—so the wave is a shallow-water long wave.
  3. Deep-ocean propagation: \(c = \sqrt{gH} \approx 200 m/s \approx 700 \mathrm{km}/h (H = 4 \mathrm{km})\); amplitude only cm–m, wavelength hundreds of km, often an N-wave. Dispersion is weak \((\lambda \gg H)\), so the shallow-water equations apply; when \(\lambda \) is comparable to H, Boussinesq/KdV corrections are needed.
  4. Coastal amplification: as H decreases, c decreases and energy-flux conservation raises the amplitude by Green’s law \(A \propto H^{-1/4}\)(with a width factor \(b^{-1/2}\) when refraction is included); refraction turns crests parallel to the coast; shoaling, run-up and inundation follow; harbour resonance and edge waves can amplify locally.
  5. Period: minutes to about an hour, typically 10–40 min.
\[c=\sqrt{gH},\qquad T=\frac\lambda c,\qquad A\propto H^{-1/4}\]

Is long-distance propagation affected by Coriolis?

Answer: to first order, no; Coriolis is a secondary effect.

Frequency criterion

\(T = 30 \operatorname{min} \to \sigma \approx 3.5\times 10^{-3} s^{-1}\); at mid-latitudes \(f \approx 1\times 10^{-4} s^{-1}\); \(\sigma /f \approx 35 \gg 1\).

Space criterion

\(\lambda = 300\text{–}500 \mathrm{km}\); barotropic \(R_{d} = \sqrt{gH}/f \approx 2000 \mathrm{km}\); \(kR_{d} \approx 25\text{–}40 \gg 1\).

So a tsunami is “high frequency” relative to the inertial period and a “short wave” relative to the deformation radius. It travels essentially as a non-rotating shallow-water wave: along great-circle paths (refracted by bathymetry), without significant deflection, trapping or dispersion by Coriolis; arrival times are controlled mainly by depth and path.

Rotation matters only for ultra-long waves with \(\sigma \lesssim f\)(period ≳ the inertial period, i.e. > 12–24 h) or \(\lambda \gtrsim 2\pi R_{d}\)(tens of thousands of km). The finite source size rarely produces such long waves.

Coriolis does play a second-order role in several ways:

  • the tsunami excites coastal Kelvin and edge waves, which are rotationally trapped and affect later wave trains, arrival times and nearshore amplification;
  • it leaves a weak geostrophic current or eddy behind (geostrophic adjustment);
  • on shallow shelves (smaller \(R_{d}\)) or at high latitudes (larger f) rotation is relatively more important;
  • near the equator \(f \to 0\), so rotation matters even less, though \(\beta \) can generate equatorial waves.
The usual confusion: “long wave” does not mean “low frequency”. A tsunami is a long wave \((\lambda \gg H)\) but its frequency is not low (T ≪ inertial period), so Coriolis can still be neglected.

Six common pitfalls

  • “Long wave” does not mean “low frequency”. A tsunami has a long \(\lambda \) but a short T; it is high-frequency relative to the inertial period, so Coriolis is weak.
  • Shallow-water gravity waves are non-dispersive, but adding rotation (Poincaré waves) makes them dispersive again. \(\sigma ^{2} = f^{2} + gHk^{2}\) reintroduces k-dependence.
  • Rossby waves are generally dispersive, but the long-wave limit is not. For \(kR \ll 1\), \(\sigma \approx -\beta R^{2}k\) and \(c = -\beta R^{2}\).
  • Kelvin waves are non-dispersive; shelf waves generally dispersive. The former has \(\sigma = kc\); the latter’s speed depends on topography and mode.
  • The equatorial waveguide width is \(\sqrt{c/\beta }\), not \(\sqrt{gH}/f\). The latter diverges at the equator and cannot describe equatorial trapping.
  • A seiche is not a different restoring force. It is still a gravity wave, merely turned into a standing wave by boundaries.

30-second retell

  1. Why can wind waves ignore Coriolis, while the M2 tide cannot?
  2. Deep-water gravity waves are dispersive and long Rossby waves are not—what is the criterion for each?
  3. What sets the width of the equatorial waveguide? Why not \(\sqrt{gH}/f\)?
  4. What do Kelvin and shelf waves have in common in propagation direction?
  5. A tsunami is a long wave—why can Coriolis still be neglected?
