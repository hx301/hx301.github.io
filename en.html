Session 1

What Is Descriptive Physical Oceanography?

2026-09-17 · Textbook Ch.1 §1.1–1.2

Driving question: The ocean is a fluid in constant motion—how do we describe it, and when does Earth's rotation start to matter?

Class notes

Descriptive physical oceanography starts from observations and model output and describes the ocean's motion and water properties as quantitatively as possible. Dynamical physical oceanography then uses theory and process experiments to explain the mechanisms. First answer where, how much, and how it changes; then ask why.

Ocean phenomena span about ten orders of magnitude in space (1 mm–10,000 km) and time (seconds–millennia). One observing design cannot resolve both surface waves and climate; the averaging interval is part of the scientific definition.

To judge whether rotation matters, compute the Rossby number Ro=U/(fL). For large-scale circulation Ro~10⁻³ and geostrophy is a good leading balance; near the equator, in boundary layers, and with strong friction, check again.

  • Description is not the absence of dynamics; it is where dynamical explanation begins.
  • Five questions for any figure: variable and units, time window, spatial domain, data source, uncertainty and gaps.
  • Data sources: in-situ observations, satellite remote sensing, historical archives, and model reanalysis.

In my own words

I used to think physical oceanography would begin with currents and equations. Instead, the first class began with how to describe: where, how much, and how it changes—then why. The scale figure from 1 mm to 10,000 km made me realize that the same approximation cannot explain both surface waves and climate.

❓ What I did not understand + ✅ How I worked it out

❓ What I did not understand at first: why does a small Rossby number guarantee geostrophic balance?
✅ What I later worked out: a small Ro means rotation dominates and advection is relatively small, but time scale, friction, and boundary layers still matter. Geostrophy is a leading balance, not the absence of acceleration.

🎫 Exit ticket

Question: Are daily-mean data enough to study temperature variability on a roughly six-hour time scale?

Think first, then reveal a reference answer

My answer: No. Daily averaging weakens or aliases the diurnal signal. Use sampling clearly shorter than six hours (for example, hourly), and check instrument response, continuous coverage, and gaps.

That ticket led to Session 2: first fix the observing scale, then ask how topography constrains the pathways of water.

Session 2

Basins, Straits, and Topography

2026-09-24 · Textbook Ch.2 §2.2–2.9

Driving question: Why is seafloor topography more than a background to current maps?

Class notes

The ocean covers about 71% of Earth's surface, with a mean depth of about 3,734 m; mean land elevation is about 840 m. The deepest point, in the Mariana Trench, is about 11,000 m.

Continental shelves, slopes, rises, abyssal plains, mid-ocean ridges, and trenches are shaped by plate tectonics and sedimentation, and they determine whether water at different depths can exchange directly.

A sill is like a threshold: it restricts deep passages but is not a sealed wall. Dense overflow water can continue to descend after mixing, or settle at a shallower level; deep renewal depends on density contrast, transport, and mixing.

  • Basins, ridges, and passages jointly set connectivity.
  • A gridded map does not mean every cell has a direct sounding.
  • Bathymetry: shipborne acoustics, satellite gravity, and GEBCO/ETOPO grids.

In my own words

I used to think seafloor topography was just background for current maps. Later I saw that sills, straits, and ridges decide which layer of water can enter. Put a 4,000 m basin next to an 800 m sill: deep does not mean closed.

❓ What I did not understand + ✅ How I worked it out

❓ What I did not understand at first: with an 800 m sill, can the deep water of a 4,000 m basin never be renewed?
✅ What I later worked out: overflow water that remains denser than the basin water after mixing can descend along the slope. Renewal depends on density contrast, transport, and mixing, not on two depth numbers.

🎫 Exit ticket

Question: To judge whether deep water on opposite sides of a ridge can exchange, which three kinds of information should you check first?

Think first, then reveal a reference answer

My answer: First, the depth and width of passages and sills; second, the temperature–salinity and density structure on both sides; third, the velocity or transport through the passage and its variability. A surface map or the basin's maximum depth is not enough.

The next session moved from where water is to what water is: temperature, salinity, and density.

Session 3

Physical Properties of Seawater

2026-10-08 · Textbook Ch.3 §3.2–3.5

Driving question: Why is temperature alone not enough to compare the buoyancy of two water parcels?

Class notes

Density depends nonlinearly on temperature, salinity, and pressure. Warming usually lowers density, adding salt raises it, and pressure compresses water; “cold water is always heavier” is not an unconditional rule.

In-situ temperature at depth includes adiabatic compression. Potential temperature compares parcels after an imaginary adiabatic move to a common reference pressure; potential density also needs a stated reference pressure.

TEOS-10 distinguishes Practical Salinity SP (conductivity-based, dimensionless) from Absolute Salinity SA (often g/kg); similar names do not make them interchangeable in code. Stratification asks whether a displaced parcel returns: N²=−(g/ρ₀)∂ρ/∂z.

  • Cabbeling: mixing two water masses with the same density but different T–S can produce denser water.
  • Double diffusion / salt fingers: heat diffuses faster than salt, forming thin finger structures.
  • SOFAR channel: a sound-speed minimum near 1,000 m lets sound travel thousands of kilometres.

In my own words

I used to think cold water must be heavier than warm water. Later I saw that salinity can reverse the conclusion: relatively fresh cold water can be lighter than salty warm water. Potential temperature and potential density also need a reference pressure; otherwise two numbers cannot be compared directly.

❓ What I did not understand + ✅ How I worked it out

❓ What I did not understand at first: if in-situ density increases with depth, isn't the stratification stable?
✅ What I later worked out: pressure compression also increases density downward. Use potential density or N² to see whether a displaced parcel returns.

🎫 Exit ticket

Question: At a common pressure, Δρ/ρ₀≈−αΔT+βΔSA. With α=2×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹, β=8×10⁻⁴ (g/kg)⁻¹, warming by 2 K and adding 0.5 g/kg of salt, what is the first-order density change?

Think first, then reveal a reference answer

My answer: −2×10⁻⁴×2 + 8×10⁻⁴×0.5 = −4×10⁻⁴ + 4×10⁻⁴ = 0. The two first-order effects cancel; this is a fixed-coefficient teaching approximation, not the full nonlinear equation of state.

The next session put temperature and salinity on real profiles to read water masses and stratification.

Session 4

Typical Distributions and Water Masses

2026-10-15 · Textbook Ch.4 §4.2–4.7

Driving question: What can extrema, slopes, and layers in a temperature–salinity profile tell us?

Class notes

The world ocean averages about 3.5 °C and 34.6 in salinity; three quarters of its volume lies between 0–6 °C and 34–35.

A typical subtropical profile has a mixed surface layer, a thermocline, and colder deep water. This is a comparison framework, not a year-round template for every region; in subpolar and polar waters, salinity may dominate stratification.

A T–S diagram compares temperature and salinity together: one point is one water type, mixing between two water masses follows a straight line, and isopycnals give the density background. Identifying a water mass requires more than one extremum: T–S relationships, density level, oxygen/nutrients, spatial continuity, and passages.

  • AABW: Antarctic shelf, about −0.5 °C, 34.7.
  • NADW: Norwegian and Labrador seas, about 2–4 °C, 34.9–35.0.
  • AAIW: north of the Antarctic Polar Front, about 2–4 °C, 34.5–34.7.

In my own words

I used to think a T–S diagram was just a scatter plot of temperature and salinity. Later I saw it as a water-mass fingerprint: mixing between two water masses falls on a straight line, and salinity can separate waters with similar temperature but different origins.

❓ What I did not understand + ✅ How I worked it out

❓ What I did not understand at first: why is surface salinity highest in the subtropics, not at the equator where evaporation is strongest?
✅ What I later worked out: salinity responds to evaporation minus precipitation (E−P). The subtropics have little rain and the largest net evaporation; the equator has strong evaporation but also strong rain.

🎫 Exit ticket

Question: Two stations both show an intermediate salinity minimum. Can you conclude they come from the same source water mass? What else should you check?

Think first, then reveal a reference answer

My answer: Not directly. Compare the T–S relationship, density level, oxygen or other tracers, and the continuous distribution and possible passages between the stations; a similar single extremum can be produced by different mixing histories.

Session 5

Mass, Salt, and Heat Budgets

2026-10-22 · Textbook Ch.5 §5.1–5.8

Driving question: How can you separate local forcing from ocean transport when a region warms or becomes saltier?

Class notes

A budget starts with a control volume, boundaries, and a sign convention: storage change equals net input plus internal sources and sinks. Volume transport is the velocity integrated across a section; surface velocity cannot represent the full depth.

Air–sea heat exchange has four terms: shortwave Qs, longwave Qb, latent Qe, and sensible Qh. With positive into the ocean, evaporative latent heat is usually a negative term.

Mixed-layer temperature also responds to advection, entrainment, and mixing; shortwave that passes through the layer is not all absorbed within it. Ignoring these terms gives ρ₀cₚh dT/dt≈Q. Freshwater exchange changes salinity and buoyancy; wind stress adds momentum and can deepen the mixed layer.

  • Define the control volume, boundaries, units, and signs first.
  • Surface fluxes and boundary transports both change storage.
  • Strong wind can increase evaporation and deepen the mixed layer at the same time; the net temperature change depends on the size of each term.

In my own words

I used to think all heat fluxes went from the ocean to the atmosphere. Later I saw that shortwave comes from the Sun into the ocean, while longwave, latent, and sensible heat are usually upward losses. One wrong sign reverses the whole budget.

❓ What I did not understand + ✅ How I worked it out

❓ What I did not understand at first: if net heat flux is downward (into the ocean), why can SST still fall?
✅ What I later worked out: surface heating is only one term; cold-water advection, entrainment, and mixed-layer deepening can spread or remove the heat.

🎫 Exit ticket

Question: A 20 m uniform mixed layer absorbs 100 W/m² for one day, neglecting advection, entrainment, and transmitted shortwave. With ρ₀=1025 kg/m³ and cₚ=4000 J/(kg·K), what is the warming?

Think first, then reveal a reference answer

My answer: ΔT = QΔt/(ρ₀cₚh) = 100×86,400/(1025×4000×20) ≈ 0.105 K. For a 100 m layer with the other values unchanged, it is about 0.021 K.

Session 6

Dynamics: Geostrophy, Ekman, and Potential Vorticity

2026-10-29 · Textbook Ch.7 §7.2–7.8

Driving question: Why do ocean currents often follow sea-level contours instead of flowing directly downhill?

Class notes

A horizontal pressure gradient first accelerates water, and the Coriolis force grows with speed and turns the motion. At large scales, weak friction, and slow evolution, the two approximately balance and produce geostrophic flow. With x east and y north: u=−(g/f)η_y, v=(g/f)η_x.

Horizontal density differences change the pressure gradient with depth through hydrostatic balance, producing thermal-wind shear; density gives relative velocity, while absolute velocity needs a reference level or observations.

Wind stress drives Ekman transport in the surface layer; wind-stress curl produces vertical motion at the base of the Ekman layer, stretching or compressing interior columns and linking to Sverdrup meridional transport and western boundary currents. Potential vorticity q=(ζ+f)/h is approximately conserved in an ideal, single-layer, frictionless, adiabatic setting.

  • The Coriolis force is always perpendicular to motion and does no work.
  • Surface Ekman flow is deflected 45°; the vertically integrated transport is deflected 90°.
  • Western boundary currents (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio) help close the basin-scale circulation.

In my own words

I used to think the Coriolis force was a real force. Later I saw that it appears only in a rotating frame, is always perpendicular to motion, and does no work; it changes direction, not speed.

❓ What I did not understand + ✅ How I worked it out

❓ What I did not understand at first: why is Ekman transport 90° to the wind while the surface flow is only 45°?
✅ What I later worked out: 45° is the surface velocity; integrating the whole Ekman layer cancels much of the deflection at different depths, leaving a net transport at 90°.

🎫 Exit ticket

Question: At 30°N, sea level rises 0.20 m eastward over 100 km with no north–south slope. With g=9.81 m/s² and f=7.292×10⁻⁵ s⁻¹, what is the geostrophic direction and speed?

Think first, then reveal a reference answer

My answer: ηx=2×10⁻⁶, u=0, v=gηx/f≈0.269 m/s, northward. The pressure-gradient acceleration is westward; the northward flow has an eastward Coriolis acceleration in the Northern Hemisphere, and the two balance.

The next session left steady balance to see how waves and tides propagate.

Session 7

Waves, Tides, and the Coast

2026-10-30 (online module 2) · Textbook Ch.8 §8.2–8.8

Driving question: Why do waves, tides, and estuarine circulation need different explanations?

Class notes

A wave propagates a disturbance and energy; water-particle motion is not the same as the speed of a crest. Surface gravity waves use gravity as the restoring force; deep- or shallow-water behavior depends on the ratio of depth to wavelength.

For linear, non-rotating shallow-water long waves, c≈√(gH). Here “shallow” is relative to wavelength, not simply a small depth on a chart.

Stable stratification supports internal waves; tides are forced periodically by the Moon and Sun, but local range and currents also depend on basin shape, depth, rotation, friction, and resonance. Estuaries often export fresher water above a compensating inflow of saltier water, but tidal mixing, runoff, and geometry change the stratification.

  • Distinguish phase speed, group velocity, and water-particle motion.
  • Spring tides relate to aligned lunar and solar forcing, not to the season.
  • A current at one instant does not represent the long-term net transport.

In my own words

I used to think a rapidly moving crest meant the same parcel of water rushed all the way to shore. Later I saw that in small-amplitude waves the particles mostly orbit near a mean position while the crest advances as a phase; breaking and net drift near the shore are a different problem.

❓ What I did not understand + ✅ How I worked it out

❓ What I did not understand at first: why is the phase-speed ratio 2 for the same long wave in 100 m and 25 m of water?
✅ What I later worked out: for shallow-water long waves c≈√(gH), so the ratio is √(100/25)=2; “shallow” is relative to wavelength, not a small depth on a chart.

🎫 Exit ticket

Question: The same long wave satisfies the linear shallow-water approximation in both 100 m and 25 m of water. Neglecting rotation, background flow, and dissipation, what is the ratio of phase speeds?

Think first, then reveal a reference answer

My answer: c₁/c₂=√(100/25)=2. With g=9.81 m/s², the speeds are about 31.3 and 15.7 m/s. This is the crest phase speed, not the net water velocity.

Session 8

Data Analysis and Observing Methods

2026-11-05 · Textbook Ch.6 §6.1–6.7

Driving question: How can an ocean analysis be understood, checked, and reproduced by someone else?

Class notes

Analysis begins with sampling and quality control: precision describes the spread of repeated measurements, accuracy describes closeness to the true value; smooth numbers do not imply the absence of systematic bias. Insufficient sampling can miss variability or alias it.

Check units, quality flags, sensor depth, missing-value codes, and sampling intervals before choosing averages, filters, or plots. EOF decomposes space–time data into spatial modes (EOFs) and time coefficients (PCs): X=UΣVᵀ, with variance fraction λᵢ/Σλ.

Removing the seasonal cycle or trend, area weighting, and standardization change the object of analysis. Report valid samples, code, errors, and sensitivity so the result can be reproduced. EOF optimizes the reconstruction of sample variance; it does not test causal mechanisms.

  • Correlation is not causation.
  • EOF1 is not mechanism 1.
  • Missing-data choices change the representativeness of the sample.

In my own words

I used to think that if EOF1 explained 90% of the variance, it explained 90% of the physical causes. Later I saw that it is only the spatial mode that best reconstructs sample variance under a given variable, region, weighting, and preprocessing; causal attribution needs independent evidence.

❓ What I did not understand + ✅ How I worked it out

❓ What I did not understand at first: if one EOF analysis removes the trend and another keeps it, and their leading variance fractions differ, is the code wrong?
✅ What I later worked out: preprocessing changes the input matrix and total variance. First align the domain, missing-data rules, weights, and scaling, then compare sensitivity.

🎫 Exit ticket

Question: Two EOF analyses differ: one removes the trend, the other keeps it, and their leading variance fractions differ. Does that alone prove a programming error?

Think first, then reveal a reference answer

My answer: No. Preprocessing changes the input matrix and total variance. First align the time–space domain, missing-data rules, weights, and scaling, then check the SVD reconstruction and numerical error. Different but explicitly stated preprocessing can be a sensitivity comparison.

In depth: the sign and limits of hydrostatic balance

With z upward and g positive, moving downward means dz < 0, so dp > 0. The sign is more useful than memorizing a minus sign; deeper water does not flow upward just because pressure is higher, because gravity participates in the vertical balance.

Link to class: when vertical acceleration is small, the hydrostatic approximation suits many large-scale problems. It does not give horizontal velocity; that needs horizontal pressure gradients and Coriolis.

Textbook Ch.7 §7.2–7.8 · Talley et al. (2011), 6th ed.

In depth: limits of geostrophy and potential vorticity

A sea-surface slope implies a horizontal pressure gradient. In the Northern Hemisphere f > 0, an eastward rise gives northward v_g; the pressure-gradient force points west and the Coriolis acceleration east. Velocity and acceleration have different units; do not add a velocity arrow as a force.

In q=(ζ+f)/H, relative vorticity, planetary vorticity, and layer thickness jointly set the layer-model PV. With no friction or external PV sources and under the layer assumptions, stretching requires absolute vorticity to increase to preserve the ratio; PV conservation does not mean local ζ is fixed.

Gulf Stream section (S7 Fig. S7.20, PDF p.37; Table S7.3, p.38): station spacing 78.0 km, latitude 38.65°N, surface eastward velocity 94.83 cm/s; the calculation assumes no motion at 3,000 m, so the curve is not a direct current-meter measurement.

1. Introduction

Algal blooms occur in lakes, reservoirs, estuaries and the ocean. They connect biological processes with physical and chemical conditions. Currents, mixing and stratification affect the light and nutrients available to algae, as well as where cells accumulate.

The animation is a conceptual explanation. Its characters, cell counts and oxygen gauge are illustrations, not environmental measurements.

2. An introduction to algae

Algae include many different groups, ranging from microscopic cells to large seaweeds. Many use sunlight to produce organic matter. Some drift with the water; others live attached to surfaces.

Common bloom-forming groups include diatoms, dinoflagellates, green algae and cyanobacteria. The organisms commonly called “blue-green algae” are actually cyanobacteria. Different groups thrive under different conditions, and only some species or strains produce toxins.

3. What is an algal bloom?

A bloom develops when algae or cyanobacteria grow or gather in large amounts, substantially increasing their local concentration or biomass.

Blooms may occur at the surface or within the water column. Their appearance varies, and some harmful events are not obvious to the eye. Water colour alone cannot establish whether toxins are present.

Nutrient supply, available light, temperature and water movement interact. Excess nitrogen and phosphorus can increase bloom risk, but no single factor explains every event.

4. Ecological roles of algae

Primary production and food webs

Algae convert light energy into chemical energy stored in organic matter. Grazers consume this production, passing energy through aquatic food webs.

Oxygen and nutrient cycling

Photosynthesis releases oxygen and growth takes up nutrients. Respiration and decomposition consume oxygen, while recycling some nutrients. Water exchange also helps determine the overall oxygen balance.

Habitats and environmental change

Large seaweeds can provide habitat and shelter. Changes in algal communities can help reveal environmental change, but water quality must be assessed using several indicators.

5. Bloom formation and impacts

Growth, losses and transport

Think of algal biomass as a budget. Growth and incoming cells add to it; grazing, death and transport away subtract from it. If gains keep exceeding losses, biomass can accumulate.

Wind and currents can also gather existing cells. A greener shoreline therefore does not necessarily mean that the whole lake contains more algae.

Shading, oxygen loss and toxins

Dense algae can reduce underwater light. When large quantities die, microbial decomposition can consume oxygen and harm fish and other aquatic life. Some species produce toxins that can affect ecosystems and people through contaminated water or seafood.

Not all blooms are harmful, and harmful blooms need not be toxic: excess biomass can cause damage through shading and oxygen depletion. Clearer water does not necessarily mean all risks have ended.

6. Prevention and management

Reduce excess nutrient inputs

Improve wastewater and agricultural nutrient management, guided by the sources and conditions of the local watershed.

Protect and restore suitable habitats

Wetlands and shoreline vegetation can intercept some inputs and support aquatic habitats. Restoration measures need to fit the local ecosystem.

Monitor and respond

Combine observations of algae with nutrients, temperature, oxygen and water movement. Test for toxins when relevant. Short-term control measures have limits and should complement long-term source management.

7. Conclusion

Blooms emerge from interactions between living organisms and their physical and chemical environment. Distinguishing growth from aggregation, and biomass effects from toxicity, helps explain both their causes and their consequences.

The goal is not to eliminate all algae. It is to reduce harmful bloom risks while protecting the functions of aquatic ecosystems.
