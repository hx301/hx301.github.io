Dynamical Oceanography: One Diagram, One System

Start with one sentence

Dynamical oceanography uses simple laws—Newton's laws and friends—to explain why the ocean flows, turns, and changes the way it does.

Descriptive physical oceanography answers what and where; dynamical oceanography answers why. This diagram is the roadmap to why: start from the simplest balance, then add one real-world factor at a time.

Two difficulties: why Newton's laws are not enough

1

The ocean is rotating

Earth spins. In a rotating frame, moving objects appear to curve—this is the Coriolis force. It is not a real force; it is inertia seen from a rotating frame.

Analogy: throw a ball straight across a spinning turntable; someone standing beside it sees the ball curve.

2

The ocean is a continuum

Seawater is not a bag of billiard balls; it is a continuous fluid. We cannot track only individual parcels—\(w_e\) describe fields: velocity, pressure, density.

Analogy: a pot of soup, not a bag of marbles.

One sentence: rotation complicates direction; the continuum complicates the mathematics. The nine steps add these complications one by one.

The nine-step chain: add one real-world factor at a time

The whole chain: geostrophy → add friction → add stratification → add vorticity → add potential vorticity → add boundaries → add wind curl → add the western boundary → add tropical air–sea coupling. Each step below follows “what is added / one sentence / a real example.”

STEP 1

Geostrophy

What is added：Keep only the pressure-gradient and Coriolis forces; ignore friction for now.

Large-scale water does not rush straight into a low; it flows along pressure contours. The pressure gradient pushes it across, and Coriolis turns it back.

Real example：The large-scale paths of the Gulf Stream and Kuroshio.

STEP 2

Geostrophy + friction → Ekman layer and upwelling

What is added：Add wind stress and friction.

Wind pushes the surface layer; rotation turns the flow to the right (Northern Hemisphere). The surface flow is deflected 45°, while the depth-integrated transport is deflected 90°.

Real example：Offshore wind pushes surface water away from the coast; cold deep water rises to replace it—upwelling.

STEP 3

Geostrophy + stratification → Gulf Stream structure and river plumes

What is added：Add density stratification: warm, light water above cold, dense water.

Tilted density surfaces make velocity change with depth (thermal wind). The Gulf Stream is not a uniform pipe; it has a baroclinic structure.

Real example：The Gulf Stream and freshwater plumes from rivers.

STEP 4

Vorticity

What is added：Start describing the local rotation of a water parcel.

Vorticity measures how fast a fluid element rotates locally; its sign tells counter-clockwise from clockwise.

Real example：Eddies and shear flows.

STEP 5

Potential vorticity

What is added：Combine vorticity, Earth's rotation, and water-column thickness: \(q=(\zeta +f)/H\).

PV is a conservation ledger of “spin + thickness.” Stretch or squeeze a column, and its spin must change.

Real example：A water column crossing a ridge or stretched by a coastal current.

STEP 6

PV conservation + coastally trapped waves

What is added：Add boundaries (coast, shelf) and topography.

The coast and topography trap waves near the boundary; Rossby, shelf, and Kelvin waves all propagate by conserving PV.

Real example：Coastal sea-level signals and flow variability.

STEP 7

Sverdrup relationship + Davidson Current

What is added：Add the curl of the wind stress—the “twist” of the wind field.

Wind-stress curl drives north–south transport in the ocean interior; Sverdrup balance links the wind field to meridional flow.

Real example：The Davidson Current off California.

STEP 8

Western boundary currents → Gulf Stream and Kuroshio

What is added：Add the western boundary of the basin.

To return the Sverdrup transport at the boundary, a narrow, fast western boundary current forms.

Real example：The Gulf Stream and Kuroshio.

STEP 9

El Niño and the Southern Oscillation (ENSO)

What is added：Add tropical air–sea coupling and climate variability.

Wind, thermocline depth, and SST in the equatorial Pacific feed back on one another, producing El Niño/La Niña.

Real example：The 1997–98 and 2015–16 El Niño events.

One main line: build it like blocks

The nine steps are not a list; they build on one another. Start with no friction (geostrophy), add wind and friction (Ekman/upwelling), add stratification (Gulf Stream/plumes), add vorticity and PV (column stretching), add boundaries (coastal waves), add wind curl (Sverdrup), add the western boundary (Gulf Stream/Kuroshio), and finally add tropical air–sea coupling (ENSO). Each added factor explains another class of phenomena; each step back returns to a simpler idealization.

Memory line: spin, wind, density; vorticity, PV, coast; wind curl, west boundary, ENSO.

One more card: what exactly is a planetary Rossby wave?

In one sentence: a planetary Rossby wave is a large-scale wave caused by the change of Earth's rotation with latitude (the \(\beta \) effect); its phase always propagates westward.

Why it exists: when a parcel moves north, f increases; to conserve potential vorticity, relative vorticity must decrease (anticyclonic). When it moves south, f decreases; relative vorticity must increase (cyclonic). This restoring effect makes the disturbance shift westward.

Parcel moves northf ↑relative vorticity \(\zeta \downarrow \)(anticyclonic)
PV conserved
\(q = (\zeta + f)/H\)
Parcel moves southf ↓relative vorticity \(\zeta \uparrow \)(cyclonic)
\[\beta=\frac{\mathrm df}{\mathrm dy}=\frac{2\Omega\cos\varphi}{a}\]
\[\frac{\partial\zeta}{\partial t}+\beta v=0\]
\[\omega=-\frac{\beta k}{k^2+l^2}\]
\[c_x=\frac{\omega}{k}=-\frac{\beta}{k^2+l^2}<0\quad\text{(westward)}\]
  • Longer waves propagate westward faster; short waves are slower.
  • A westward phase speed does not mean energy can only go west: the group velocity of short waves can be eastward.
  • In the ocean, the first baroclinic mode travels at a few \(\mathrm{cm}/s\), taking months to years to cross a basin; in the atmosphere, it appears as the long-wave troughs and ridges of the westerlies.
  • Equatorial Rossby waves carry western-Pacific wind anomalies westward and reflect into Kelvin waves—an important part of ENSO; coastal Rossby and shelf waves are trapped by the boundary.

Analogy: like a row of dominoes—each tile falls only once, but the falling pattern travels far along the row. In a Rossby wave, parcels mainly move north–south while the phase moves west.

So the Rossby wave in Step 6 is not a different wave: it is what PV conservation produces when Earth's rotation changes with latitude. Coastal trapping simply ties that wave to the boundary.
